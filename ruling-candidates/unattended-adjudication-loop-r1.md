# Ruling candidate: the unattended adjudication loop R1

Status: **PROPOSED ONLY — NOT YET A RATIFIED RULING OR PROJECT RUNTIME AUTHORITY**

Origin: DNG Auto Processor, 2026-08-30. An owner directive to work autonomously "without me in the
loop" across a full day. Supporting measurements and the failures are in
`dng-auto-processor/receipts/OPUS-UNATTENDED-ADJUDICATION-LOOP-20260830.md`.

## The problem this addresses

An autonomous producer outruns its reviewer. The reviewer is then either skipped, or run by the
producer's own vendor and quota, so producer exhaustion silences the gate exactly when the producer is
generating most. Measured at origin: a weekly provider pool hit 100%, and every review gate went down
with the producer because both were the same vendor. Nineteen days had passed with zero adoption gates
closed and a review backlog that grew 13 → 71 in thirty hours.

## Proposed portable law

**1. Put the terminating gate on a different vendor from the producer.** Not merely a different model
— a different provider, quota and failure domain. This buys two independent properties: the chain
terminates outside the vendor that produced the work, and producer exhaustion cannot silence review.
Both matter; the second is the one nobody plans for.

**2. The producer chooses the SUBJECT. It must not choose the QUESTION.** Build every routine review
brief by mechanical substitution into a fixed template, and classify the objective's origin explicitly:

| origin of the objective | independence claim |
|---|---|
| generated by the routing system | strongest |
| a fixed stage objective, asked identically of every subject at that stage | strong |
| the subject's own producer, written at build time | ordinary review; may frame favourably |
| the reviewer, written beside the subject it is about to judge | **none — must not qualify as a ruling** |

Record which tier applied in the run receipt. Hashing an objective proves integrity, never
independence: a hash of a question you wrote yourself is a hash of your own choice.

**3. A run is a ruling only if a receipt says so.** Bind, per run: provider, authentication domain,
executable, model, effort, sandbox, the subject SHA **before and after** the run, the template and
rendered-brief SHAs, the output SHA, and the objective's origin tier. Set one boolean that is false on
non-zero exit, output that fails its schema, an ad-hoc brief, **or a subject whose SHA changed during
review**. Consumers check the boolean, not the exit code.

**4. Acceptance is not terminal at the first gate.** Run S1 (adjudicate) → L1 (falsify **S1's
synthesis**, mandatory only when S1 accepted) → S2 (adjudicate the triple; terminal). A second reviewer
asked the *same question* is redundancy; falsification must be generated from the position it attacks.
Measured at origin: the only ACCEPT produced all day was overturned at L1 for a check-then-write race,
and S2 confirmed the refutation. Under a design where S1 was terminal, that would have shipped.

**5. Sandbox the reviewer read-only — and know what that costs.** It converts "the reviewer never
mutates product bytes" from a promise into a boundary. It also makes the gate structurally unable to
execute a verifier, so every "verifier PASS N/N" claim becomes one the gate can only reason about
lexically. Make read-only-by-construction verification the default for deterministic claims over
pinned bytes; reserve an isolated writable harness for claims that inherently concern execution.

**6. Every check ships proven in both directions.** Before trusting a check, demonstrate an input that
makes it fail and an input that makes it pass. A check that has only ever returned one answer has not
been tested. At origin a coherence check counted a field name the schema did not use, silently
discarding fourteen substantive rulings; a sibling check reads a field that still does not exist and
therefore cannot fire.

**7. Measure; do not relay.** A green quoted verbatim from an upstream record is not evidence. Build
the predicate that could turn it red, then look. At origin the day's headline — "the first review gate
closed in 25 days" — was retracted once such a predicate existed: the verdict was 73% authored by a
model other than the one it was receipted as.

**8. Bound the rounds, and stop early rather than late.** Three review rounds on one artifact. At the
ceiling, keep the best artifact and split the unclosed dimension into separate work. A persuasive
argument that *this* round is categorically different is itself the failure the ceiling exists to
catch — the distinction between "a new approach" and "a fourth attempt" is not reliably available to
the seat making it.

**9. State the predicate, including the inclusion rule and the projection.** A count derived from a
truncated projection or an undeclared exclusion is not a census. At origin the same seat made both
errors within an hour, the second while repairing the first, and one of them had manufactured the
conclusion the proposal rested on.

## What an adopter owes before adopting

1. That your producer and gate genuinely have separate quota domains — verify, do not assume from
   vendor names.
2. That your reviewer's sandbox is enforced by the runtime, not by its prompt.
3. A negative control for every check introduced under law 6, retained in the repository.
4. An honest accounting of what the loop did NOT close. At origin: adoption gates remained at zero
   throughout, and the review backlog still grew faster than the gate drained it. **This law improves
   the trustworthiness of review. It does not by itself make a factory ship.**

## Honest limits

One project, one day, one owner directive. The failure modes above are measured at origin; the law is
inferred from them and is not yet demonstrated to transfer. Laws 1–4 rest on repeated observation;
laws 5–9 are each grounded in a single measured incident and should be treated as hypotheses an
adopter tests rather than conclusions an adopter inherits.
